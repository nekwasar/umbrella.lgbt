# How to Change Your Name and Gender Marker

For trans and non-binary people, changing your name and gender marker is one of the most practical — and most bureaucratic — parts of transition. It's paperwork, but it's paperwork that changes how the world sees you. Here's the complete path.

## Step 1: Choose Your Name

Before the paperwork, the name. Try it on: in online spaces, with trusted friends, in coffee shop orders (a time-honored test). Make sure it's the name you want on legal documents — changing it twice is twice the paperwork.

## Step 2: The Legal Name Change

**United States (state-dependent):**
- File a petition in your county court. Fees range $100-400; most courts offer fee waivers for low income.
- Some states require a hearing; many are paper-only.
- Many states allow a waiver of the public-notice requirement for trans people (safety reasons) — ask the clerk.
- Result: a court order — the master key for updating everything else.

**United Kingdom:**
- A deed poll (free, via a template service or notary) or statutory declaration. Simple, quick, valid nationwide.
- Gender marker is separate (see below), but passports and driving licenses can be updated with a doctor's letter.

**Other countries vary widely**: some require medical documentation; others (Germany since 2024) allow simple self-declaration. Check the current process where you live — laws in this area are changing fast.

## Step 3: The Gender Marker

- **US passports**: M/F/X markers available with self-attestation or a doctor's letter (policies vary by administration — check current rules).
- **US state IDs**: Vary enormously — some states offer X, some don't allow changes, some require medical documentation.
- **UK**: Passports and licenses updated with a doctor's letter; X marker available on passports.
- **EU/Canada/Australia**: Most allow X markers or self-declaration, with regional variations.

## Step 4: Update Everything (The Checklist)

Order matters — government documents first, then everything downstream:

1. Passport
2. Driver's license / state ID
3. Social Security record (US) / National Insurance (UK)
4. Bank accounts, credit cards
5. Employer: payroll, email, badges
6. Insurance: health, car, home
7. Medical records: doctor, dentist, pharmacy
8. School: records, transcripts, diploma options
9. Landlord / mortgage
10. Subscriptions: utilities, streaming, delivery services

Work the list top-down; each government document makes the next one easier.

## Resources That Help

- **TLDEF (US)**: Free name-change clinics in NY and NJ; online name-change guides for all states
- **Lambda Legal (US)**: Legal guidance and referrals
- **UK**: UK Deed Poll service; Gendered Intelligence's document-change guides
- **TransHub (Australia)**: State-by-state guides
- **Community centers**: Most run document-change workshops — one session with a community organizer can save you weeks

## The Reality Check

This process is genuinely frustrating — forms, fees, waiting rooms, and the occasional rude clerk. It's also worth it: every document with your correct name is a document that stops deadnaming you. Take it one form at a time, celebrate each small win, and remember that thousands of trans people have pushed this exact rock up this exact hill before you.
