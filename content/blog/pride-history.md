# Pride History: From Stonewall to Today

Pride Month isn't a corporate rainbow discount. It's a commemoration of a riot — and a movement that turned resistance into liberation. Understanding where Pride came from changes what it means.

## Before Stonewall

Queer life before the late 1960s was survival. Being gay was illegal almost everywhere in the US; bars could be raided, patrons arrested, and careers destroyed by a single outing. Police routinely raided gay bars, enforcing "morality" laws. Even in the most accepting cities, queer people lived in shadow.

There were early movements: the Mattachine Society (1950), Daughters of Bilitis (1955), and the first public gay rights demonstrations in Philadelphia (1965). But these were careful, conservative — respectable picketing with dress codes.

## Stonewall: June 28, 1969

At 1:20 AM, police raided the Stonewall Inn, a mafia-run bar in Greenwich Village that was nonetheless a refuge for the city's most marginalized queers — drag queens, butch lesbians, homeless youth, trans women, and people of color. Raids were routine. This time, the patrons fought back.

What began as resistance at the bar became days of protest across the Village. Led largely by trans women of color — Marsha P. Johnson, Sylvia Rivera — and drag queens, the Stonewall Uprising turned years of quiet endurance into public confrontation.

## The First Pride

One year later, on the anniversary, activists in New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, and San Francisco held "Christopher Street Liberation Day" marches. The name "Pride" was chosen deliberately — chosen to counter the shame that had been used to control queer people for generations.

By the 1970s, Pride marches existed in most American cities. By the 1980s, the AIDS crisis made Pride a funeral and a protest at once — ACT UP's "Silence = Death" banners framed the era.

## The Movement Since

- **1973**: Homosexuality removed from the DSM
- **2003**: US Supreme Court strikes down sodomy laws
- **2015**: Same-sex marriage legalized nationwide (US)
- **2010s-20s**: Trans visibility exploded; anti-discrimination protections expanded; then, in parts of the US and beyond, new legislative attacks emerged

Pride has been wins and losses, celebration and vigilance, in every era.

## Pride Today

Modern Pride is complicated — and that's okay. It's a massive celebration (São Paulo, New York, and Madrid draw millions) and increasingly a corporate event. But it's also:

- A protest: Trans Pride marches, Dyke Marches, and radical Pride events keep the original flame
- A family event: queer families celebrate openly in ways impossible a generation ago
- A lifeline: for closeted people in hostile places, Pride visibility says "you exist, and there are people like you"

## What Pride Means Now

Pride Month is in June because Stonewall was in June. The rainbow is a flag because Gilbert Baker made it one in 1978 — pink for sex, red for life, orange for healing, yellow for sun, green for nature, blue for art, violet for spirit. Every element is intentional.

The most important thing to know: Pride isn't over. It never ends. Every Pride month is a reminder that the work continues — in elections, in classrooms, in bathrooms, in courtrooms, and in the simple act of being visibly, unapologetically yourself.
